\begin{abstract}
An algebraic number is not made expressible by radicals merely by locating a
small-degree polynomial factor over another algebraic field: the coefficient
field must itself have a radical description, and the desired complex embedding
must survive every conversion. This article turns the experiments in
\texttt{FindExtension.nb} into a layered implementation. Cheap structural
recognizers and optional extension hints handle favorable inputs; an explicit
splitting-field algorithm supplies the general constructive route. Its essential
steps are exact embedded automorphisms, a prime-index subnormal series,
nonvanishing Fourier resolvents, rational coordinate reconstruction, and exact
principal-branch tests. A finite procedure for the unrestricted mathematical
problem is proved. The shipped code implements that procedure using SymPy,
with explicit resource guards and without claiming practical performance on
all solvable inputs. Successful results are radical straight-line programs,
not disguised \texttt{Root} objects or trigonometric constants. The Wolfram
interface independently checks each returned expression against the original
input with \texttt{RootReduce}.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem and the contract}
\label{sec:contract}
The motivating question asks why \code{ToRadicals} sometimes leaves an exact
\code{Root} unchanged even though a radical expression exists
\cite{question}. Wolfram documents precisely this possibility, while guaranteeing
conversion for polynomial roots of degree at most four \cite{toradicals}.
The useful goal is therefore not to wrap \code{ToRadicals} in more simplification
rules, but to supply the missing construction.

The input to the core algorithm is a specified algebraic number
\(\alpha\in\overline{\Q}\subset\C\), with its complex embedding. Let
\(f\in\Q[x]\) be its monic minimal polynomial. A successful result is a finite
expression generated from rational numbers by addition, multiplication,
inversion, and rational powers with their ordinary principal complex meanings.
The implementation also admits \(i\), which is itself \((-1)^{1/2}\).
Parameter-dependent algebraic functions, approximate numbers, and arbitrary
transcendental constants are outside this contract.

This formulation matters in three ways. First, the input polynomial need not
be irreducible: only the factor belonging to the selected root is relevant.
Second, specifying a polynomial without its root embedding is insufficient to
select one radical value. Third, finding an expression for some conjugate is
not success for the original \code{Root}.

The public result distinguishes the following outcomes.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{.18\textwidth}p{.74\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Python status & Meaning \\\midrule
\code{success} & A radical expression has passed an exact algebraic verification.\\
\code{not_solvable} & An exact obstruction proves that the minimal polynomial has a nonsolvable Galois group.\\
\code{resource_limit} & A configured degree or worker-time limit was reached. No conclusion about solvability follows.\\
\code{unknown} & A bounded recognizer failed, a backend computation failed, or verification was inconclusive.\\
\code{invalid_input} & The JSON request was malformed or violated the input contract. The direct Python API raises validation exceptions instead.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The Wolfram association uses corresponding capitalized status names. Its
\code{RootToRadicals} convenience function returns an expression only on success;
otherwise it returns a \code{Failure} carrying the structured result.

\subsection{What ``any solvable Root'' means here}
There is a finite, general mathematical algorithm. Its proof is given below,
and the explicit-field backend is an implementation of that algorithm rather
than a placeholder requiring an unimplemented Galois oracle. It does not rely
on a finite table of transitive groups. However, it constructs a splitting
field as an absolute number field and can be extremely expensive. A default
field-degree limit of 64 and a CLI worker limit of 180 seconds deliberately
make the default invocation a resource-bounded procedure.

Removing those limits removes these policy cutoffs; it does not provide a
practical time bound, eliminate expression swell, or establish that every
underlying CAS routine is bug-free. The completeness theorem is about the
algorithm under terminating exact algebraic primitives. The validation claims
are about the concrete inputs actually executed.

\section{What the notebook contributes}
\label{sec:notebook}
The supplied archive contains \code{FindExtension.nb}. Its input cells include
several complementary searches rather than a single closed algorithm.
\code{findExtension} imposes divisibility by a generic quadratic
\(x^2+px+q\), eliminates variables, and tries to select a useful coefficient.
\code{findExtension3} repeats the idea with a cubic factor.
\code{project} tests whether real or imaginary parts of conjugates have lower
minimal-polynomial degree. The \code{factor} and \code{solve} helpers then
select factors or roots using \code{PossibleZeroQ}. Finally, \code{fuzz}
searches sums of randomly selected conjugates, reconstructing candidate
algebraic numbers from high-precision approximations.

These are good ways to discover structure. A coefficient of a factor over an
extension field is an invariant of a subset of roots, hence a natural
resolvent value. Lower-degree real or imaginary projections can expose
subfields. Numerically guessed sums can reveal useful fixed-field generators.
The notebook also contains manually chosen radical and cyclotomic extensions,
and several much larger inverse-beta-related examples.

The missing invariant is a \emph{certified, decreasing field-theoretic task}.
An algebraic coefficient of degree three may be easy to radicalize, but a
coefficient of degree five may not be. Repeatedly choosing a smaller-looking
coefficient need not produce a terminating chain. A low-degree factorization
also does not specify the correct principal branches of its formulas.

The implementation retains the useful part as \emph{optional extension hints}.
Hints must already be radical expressions. Factoring over the field they
generate is exact, and every proposed answer is verified independently.
The general route replaces random subset searches with traces and character
projectors on an explicitly computed normal extension. At a prime-index step,
a nonzero projector is guaranteed to occur among a finite power-basis list.

Some notebook details should not be promoted into a package contract:
selection by \code{[[1]]} assumes a nonempty candidate list; \code{fuzz} has an
unbounded search loop; \code{PossibleZeroQ} is not used here as an equality
certificate; and one complexity-function name appears as
\code{Simplify`SimpifyCount}. The delivered code avoids undocumented simplifier
complexity functions. It does not evaluate the original notebook or claim that
all its large examples have been solved. A source manifest and selected
regression polynomials are included in \code{notes/}.

\section{Structural reductions that avoid the splitting field}
\label{sec:structural}
A general algorithm should not make a sextic pay for a degree-24 splitting
field when a cubic substitution will do. Recognition is performed over
\(\Q\), with exact coefficient comparisons. Candidate ordering may use
approximations, but acceptance does not.

\subsection{Translation and pure powers}
For a monic polynomial of degree \(n\), translation
\(x=y-c_{n-1}/n\) removes the next-to-leading coefficient. If the result is
\(y^n-b\), enumerate
\[
 y_k=\zeta_n^k b^{1/n},\qquad 0\leq k<n,
 \qquad \zeta_n=(-1)^{2/n}.
\]
The branch of \(b^{1/n}\) is principal, and the root-of-unity factors enumerate
all alternatives. In particular, a negative real radicand of odd order is not
silently interpreted as a real root by the complex-power operation.

If all nonconstant exponents have a common divisor \(g>1\), solve the reduced
polynomial in \(y^g\), and again enumerate every phase of each \(g\)-th root.
The Python recognizer also handles rational polynomial compositions when all
inner components can be inverted in degree at most four.

\subsection{Dickson polynomials and coupled radicals}
Define
\[
 D_0(x,a)=2,\quad D_1(x,a)=x,\quad
 D_n(x,a)=xD_{n-1}(x,a)-aD_{n-2}(x,a).
\]
Induction gives the central identity
\begin{equation}
 D_n(u+a/u,a)=u^n+(a/u)^n.\label{eq:dickson}
\end{equation}
For \(D_n(x,a)=b\), solve
\(U^2-bU+a^n=0\), take one nonzero solution \(U\), and put
\[
 u=U^{1/n},\qquad
 x_k=\zeta_n^k u+\frac{a}{\zeta_n^k u}.
\]
When \(a\ne0\), the chosen \(U\) is nonzero. The two summands are coupled by
\(uv=a\). Choosing two unrelated principal \(n\)-th roots instead can break
that condition and produce an incorrect answer.

For the quintic in the question,
\[
 5x^5-25x^3+25x+6=5\left(D_5(x,1)+\frac65\right).
\]
Thus, with all powers principal,
\begin{equation}
 u=\left(\frac{-3+4i}{5}\right)^{1/5},\qquad
 \boxed{\alpha=u+u^{-1}}.\label{eq:quintic}
\end{equation}
Since \(u\) has unit modulus and argument between zero and \(\pi/5\), this is
the largest real root, namely the question's Wolfram index 5. Its other roots
are obtained by the same phase enumeration. The radical expression was
verified against the specified algebraic number, not only substituted into
the quintic.

\subsection{Generalized reciprocal reductions}
Suppose a monic polynomial of degree \(2d\) has coefficients satisfying
\[
 c_{d-j}=a^j c_{d+j}\qquad(1\leq j\leq d),\quad a\in\Q^\times.
\]
In particular, \(c_0=a^d\), so there are at most two rational candidates for
\(a\). Then
\begin{equation}
 f(x)=x^d h(x+a/x),\qquad
 h(t)=c_d+\sum_{j=1}^d c_{d+j}D_j(t,a).\label{eq:reciprocal}
\end{equation}
After solving \(h(t)=0\), recover
\(x=(t\pm\sqrt{t^2-4a})/2\). Both signs must be retained until the selected
embedding is certified. The familiar reciprocal substitution \(x+1/x\)
is just the case \(a=1\); allowing \(a=-1\) is essential for the sextic.

Indeed,
\begin{align}
 f(x)&=x^6+x^4-x^3-x^2-1\notag\\
 &=x^3\left((x-1/x)^3+4(x-1/x)-1\right).\label{eq:sexticreduce}
\end{align}
Let
\begin{equation}
 v=\left(\frac{9+\sqrt{849}}{18}\right)^{1/3},\qquad
 t=v-\frac{4}{3v}.
\end{equation}
Then \(t^3+4t-1=0\). The cubic is strictly increasing on the real line,
so it has exactly one real root. The sextic therefore has exactly two real
roots, one of each sign, and the positive one is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\alpha=\frac{t+\sqrt{t^2+4}}2}.\label{eq:sextic}
\end{equation}
This is the original Wolfram \code{Root[...,2]}. It needs only a cubic radical
and square roots, with no auxiliary search for a general quartic field.

\subsection{Cyclotomic expressions without hidden trigonometry}
For a cyclotomic polynomial \(\Phi_m\), emit
\(\zeta_m^k=(-1)^{2k/m}\) for \(\gcd(k,m)=1\). This is an actual radical
expression under the contract; neither \(\cos(2\pi/m)\) nor a new
\code{Root} is retained in the output.

The Python recognizer also detects maximal real cyclotomic fields. For
\(m\ge3\), eliminating \(z\) between \(\Phi_m(z)=0\) and
\(z^2-tz+1=0\) gives the square of the minimal polynomial of
\(\zeta_m+\zeta_m^{-1}\). The square appears because \(k\) and \(-k\) give
the same trace. An exact squarefree-part comparison recognizes the polynomial
and emits these traces using powers of \(-1\).

The searches are finite. From
\[
 \frac{\varphi(m)^2}{m}
   =\prod_{p^e\Vert m}p^{e-2}(p-1)^2\ge\frac12
\]
we have \(m\le2n^2\) when \(\varphi(m)=n\), and \(m\le8n^2\) when
\(\varphi(m)=2n\). Every factor on the left is at least one except possibly
the factor for \(2^1\), which is \(1/2\). This proves the bounds used by the
recognizers. These searches recognize cyclotomic generators and their maximal
real traces, not arbitrary generators of every abelian extension.

\section{The exact obstruction: normal closure, not root-field automorphisms}
\label{sec:galois}
Let \(L\) be the splitting field of the minimal polynomial \(f\), in the
chosen algebraic closure, and \(G=\Gal(L/\Q)\).
The classical criterion is
\begin{equation}
 \alpha\text{ is expressible by radicals over }\Q
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G\text{ is solvable}.\label{eq:criterion}
\end{equation}
For background on radical extensions, normal closures, and the Galois
correspondence, see Milne \cite{milne}. This criterion concerns the minimal
polynomial of the \emph{particular input}, not every irreducible factor of a
possibly larger input polynomial.

A frequent wrong shortcut is to compute
\(\operatorname{Aut}(\Q(\alpha)/\Q)\) instead of the Galois group of its
normal closure. For an irreducible polynomial that automorphism group can be
trivial even when the normal closure has a large nonsolvable group. A second
wrong shortcut is to infer radical solvability merely from the degree being a
product of small primes. Degree alone supplies no such criterion.

For completeness, the necessity in \eqref{eq:criterion} can be understood by
normalizing a radical tower: adjoin the necessary roots of unity and all
conjugates of the successive radicals. The resulting normal extensions have
abelian successive kernels. Their composite has a solvable Galois group, and
so does the quotient acting on the splitting field of \(f\). Conversely, the
construction in the next sections explicitly realizes a solvable normal
extension by a radical tower.

\subsection{An exact inexpensive negative test in prime degree}
\begin{proposition}
Let \(f\in\Q[x]\) be irreducible of prime degree \(n\). At a prime of good
squarefree reduction, the degrees of its irreducible modular factors are the
cycle lengths of an element of its Galois group. If those lengths are not of
one of the forms
\[
 1^n,\qquad n,\qquad 1\,d^{(n-1)/d}\quad(d>1,\ d\mid n-1),
\]
then \(f\) is not solvable by radicals.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A solvable transitive group of prime degree has a minimal nontrivial normal
subgroup \(N\) that is elementary abelian. Normal orbits are blocks, so \(N\)
is transitive. An abelian transitive permutation group is regular: a point
stabilizer fixes every point, hence is trivial in this faithful action.
Thus \(|N|=n\) and \(N\simeq C_n\). The full group lies in the normalizer
of this regular cyclic subgroup, namely the affine group
\(x\mapsto ax+b\) of \(\mathbb F_n\). Translations have an \(n\)-cycle or
are the identity. For \(a\ne1\), there is one fixed point and every other
orbit has length the multiplicative order of \(a\). Combining this
classification with the good-prime Frobenius factorization theorem proves
the claim.
\end{proof}
The implementation tries twelve small rational primes. A failure to find an
obstruction means nothing negative. For example,
\[
 x^5-x-1\equiv(x^2+x+1)(x^3+x^2+1)\pmod2
\]
is squarefree and has cycle type \((2,3)\), which excludes all solvable
transitive quintic groups. For \(x^7-x-1\), reduction modulo 3 gives factor
degrees \((2,5)\), likewise an obstruction. Irreducibility is not guessed:
the caller has already replaced the input by its minimal polynomial.

The optional SymPy \code{galois_group} call is a second negative shortcut for
degrees at most six, its documented range \cite{sympyfields}. Its returned
abstract permutations are never applied to a numerically sorted list of roots.
The constructive backend computes an embedded action itself.

\section{An explicit general construction}
\label{sec:construction}
\subsection{Building a normal field and its embedded automorphisms}
The reference backend enumerates all roots of \(f\) exactly and successively
adjoins roots not already in the current field. This gives the splitting
field \(L\). Containment and annihilation of all distinct roots are checked.
The use of a primitive element lets every field element be represented by a
rational coefficient vector modulo one irreducible polynomial.

Write \(g=[L:\Q]=|G|\), and set
\begin{equation}
 m=\rad(g)=\prod_{p\mid g}p,\qquad
 B=\Q(\zeta_m),\qquad M=LB.\label{eq:cyclobase}
\end{equation}
Here \(M\) is again normal over \(\Q\). Represent it as
\(\Q(\theta)\), with \(D=[M:\Q]\) and monic minimal polynomial \(P\) of
\(\theta\). Factoring \(P\) over \(M\) must give exactly \(D\) distinct
linear factors. Their roots are precisely the possible images of \(\theta\)
under all \(\Q\)-automorphisms of \(M\).

For each such image \(\theta'\), the automorphism acts by reduced Horner
substitution:
\[
 \sigma_{\theta'}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}q_j\theta^j\right)
       =\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}q_j(\theta')^j.
\]
Keep the automorphisms fixing \(\zeta_m\). They form
\(H=\Gal(M/B)\). Their composition table is computed by applying one
substitution to the other's image of \(\theta\); closure, identity, inverses,
and the degree equality \(|H|\varphi(m)=D\) are checked.

\begin{lemma}
\(H\) is solvable if and only if \(G\) is solvable. Every prime dividing
\(|H|\) divides \(m\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Restriction identifies \(H\) with \(\Gal(L/L\cap B)\), a subgroup of
\(G\). Since \(B/\Q\) is abelian, \((L\cap B)/\Q\) is abelian and
Galois. Thus this subgroup is normal, with abelian quotient. A group is
solvable precisely when such a normal subgroup is solvable. Its order divides
\(|G|\), proving the assertion about primes.
\end{proof}
This avoids an unnecessary recursive expansion of the cyclotomic base.
The output language already expresses \(\zeta_m\) by the single rational
power \((-1)^{2/m}\). Primes dividing \(\varphi(m)\) do not need to be
added merely to provide a separate prime-radical decomposition of that
constant. The algorithm is not minimizing the degree of \(M\).

\subsection{Constructing a prime-index series without subgroup enumeration}
For a finite solvable group \(H\), compute its commutator subgroup \(H'\).
Every subgroup containing \(H'\) is normal, because \(H/H'\) is abelian.
Starting at \(H'\), add an element whenever the generated subgroup remains
proper in \(H\). When no further addition is possible, the resulting \(N\)
is maximal among proper subgroups containing \(H'\). Consequently
\(H/N\) is cyclic of prime order.

Repeat inside \(N\), obtaining
\begin{equation}
 H=H_0\triangleright H_1\triangleright\cdots\triangleright H_s=1,
 \qquad [H_{j-1}:H_j]=p_j\text{ prime}.\label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
Each subgroup is normal in its immediate predecessor; normality in the entire
original group is not required. If a nontrivial subgroup equals its own
commutator subgroup, the derived series has stabilized nontrivially and the
algorithm proves nonsolvability instead.

\subsection{The nonzero Fourier resolvent}
Fix one step \(H_0\triangleright N\), of index \(p\), and put
\(K=M^{H_0}\), \(E=M^N\). Then \(E/K\) is cyclic of degree \(p\), and
\(\zeta_p=\zeta_m^{m/p}\in B\subset K\). Choose
\(\sigma\in H_0\setminus N\). Its restriction generates \(\Gal(E/K)\).

For \(0\le k<D\), form
\begin{equation}
 T_k=\sum_{\tau\in N}\tau(\theta^k),\qquad
 R_k=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}\zeta_p^{-j}\sigma^j(T_k).\label{eq:projector}
\end{equation}
This is a finite, deterministic replacement for random sums of roots.

\begin{lemma}
At least one \(R_k\) is nonzero. Every nonzero \(R=R_k\) satisfies
\[
 \sigma(R)=\zeta_pR,\qquad R^p\in K,\qquad E=K(R).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The trace from \(M\) to \(E\) is a surjective \(\Q\)-linear map: on \(E\)
it is multiplication by \(|N|\). Hence the \(T_k\) span \(E\) over
\(\Q\). The character projector
\(\sum_j\zeta_p^{-j}\sigma^j\) is not the zero map on \(E\): distinct
field automorphisms are linearly independent over the field. Thus it cannot
vanish on all the spanning vectors \(T_k\).

For any \(T_k\in E\), \(\sigma^p(T_k)=T_k\), so shifting the sum gives
\(\sigma(R)=\zeta_pR\). Therefore \(R^p\) is fixed by \(\sigma\) and
by \(N\), hence lies in \(K\). Since \(R\ne0\) and \(\zeta_p\ne1\),
\(R\notin K\). The prime extension degree forces \(K(R)=E\).
\end{proof}
This is the classical cyclic-to-radical construction in an explicit embedded
field. Related, substantially more efficient Galois-based constructions are
developed by Fieker and Sutherland \cite{fiekersutherland}. The present backend
does not implement their local reconstruction machinery; it deliberately uses
exact absolute-field arithmetic to make the construction executable with its
single dependency.

\subsection{Reconstructing the radicand and the final answer}
Maintain a \(\Q\)-basis \(b_1,\ldots,b_e\) of the current fixed field and
a parallel list of rational expressions in already emitted radical registers.
Initially this is
\[
 1,\zeta_m,\ldots,\zeta_m^{\varphi(m)-1}.
\]
After finding \(R\), express \(a=R^p\) in that basis by exact rational
linear algebra. Coefficient vectors are stored in the absolute power basis
of \(\theta\); solving the resulting rectangular linear system gives the
unique rational coefficients of \(a\).

Choose the principal root \(C=a^{1/p}\) among the values
\(R\zeta_p^{-k}\), as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:branches}. Since
\(\zeta_p\in K\), replacing \(R\) by \(C\) does not change the field it
generates. Emit a register assignment \(r_j=a^{1/p}\), and replace the basis by
\[
 \{b_i C^k:1\le i\le e,\ 0\le k<p\}.
\]
This is a basis of the next field: \([E:K]=p\). At the end of the series,
the basis spans all of \(M\), so the target \(\alpha\) has unique rational
coordinates in it. Substituting the parallel register expressions gives the
radical output.

\subsection{Finite algorithm and correctness}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:complete}
Assuming terminating exact algorithms for algebraic-number representations,
number-field arithmetic and factorization, rational linear algebra, and
algebraic root isolation, the unrestricted explicit-field construction decides
whether a specified \(\alpha\in\overline{\Q}\) is expressible by radicals.
On a positive input it constructs a principal-branch radical straight-line
program equal to that exact embedding of \(\alpha\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
There are finitely many roots to adjoin, so the normal field is obtained after
finitely many number-field operations. The primitive polynomial has finite
degree \(D\), so its factorization enumerates all automorphisms. Finite-group
closure and commutator computations terminate. A nontrivial stable derived
subgroup is an exact negative certificate, by the equivalence with \(G\).

Otherwise the strictly decreasing series \eqref{eq:chain} is finite.
At each step, the projector lemma guarantees success among at most \(D\)
traces. Rational coordinate reconstruction succeeds because the radicand lies
in the current fixed field. The branch tests below terminate, including on
the boundary rays. The basis invariant then proves that the last coordinate
reconstruction yields exactly \(\alpha\). Conversely, the nonsolvability
criterion excludes a radical expression in the negative case.
\end{proof}
The proof does not assert a useful polynomial-time bound. Nor does it assert
that the resulting expression is shortest, has least radical depth, or lies
in a minimal-degree radical extension. These are separate optimization tasks.

\section{Principal branches and exact certification}
\label{sec:branches}
\subsection{Why a radicand equation alone is insufficient}
An algebraic identity \(R^p=a\) provides \(p\) possible values, not a proof
that \(R\) is the value of the expression \(a^{1/p}\). The ambiguity is
particularly visible for \(p=3\) and \(a=-1\): the real root \(-1\) is
not the principal complex cube root.

For nonzero \(a\), the principal \(p\)-th root is characterized by
\begin{equation}
 C^p=a,\qquad -\frac\pi p<\Arg C\le\frac\pi p.\label{eq:sector}
\end{equation}
The asymmetry of the two boundary inequalities is intentional.

For \(p=2\), the exact sector predicate is
\[
 \Re C>0\quad\text{or}\quad(\Re C=0\text{ and }\Im C>0).
\]
For an odd prime, let
\(\eta=-\zeta_p^{(p+1)/2}=e^{\pi i/p}\). Then \eqref{eq:sector} is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
 \Im(C\eta)>0\quad\text{and}\quad\Im(C/\eta)\le0.\label{eq:sectoralgebraic}
\end{equation}
To verify this, intersect the two open/closed half-planes after rotation.
Their intersection is exactly the sector \((-\pi/p,\pi/p]\); there is no
second component at the negative real axis. No transcendental argument
function is evaluated by the implementation.

\subsection{Zero detection before interval refinement}
All field elements lie in the normal field \(M\), so complex conjugation is
an exact automorphism. For \(z\in M\),
\[
 \Re z=0\iff z+\bar z=0,\qquad
 \Im z=0\iff z-\bar z=0.
\]
These tests are performed by rational coefficient-vector equality. Only a
component proved nonzero is passed to a sign-refinement loop.

The primitive embedding \(\theta\) is isolated as an exact algebraic root.
Rational rectangles enclosing it are refined, and a polynomial expression in
\(\theta\) is evaluated by interval Horner arithmetic. Interval multiplication
uses all endpoint products; complex multiplication uses the usual real and
imaginary formulas with outward rational intervals. No machine rounding is
involved. Because the component is known to be nonzero and the rectangles
shrink to \(\theta\), the enclosing component interval eventually lies
strictly on one side of zero.

This order of operations is crucial. A pure interval procedure may refine
forever on the positive boundary ray of \eqref{eq:sector}, where one component
is exactly zero. The tests include both boundary rays for cube roots and
both imaginary-axis choices for square roots.

\subsection{Two positive verification paths}
\paragraph{Structural candidates.}
For a proposed expression \(e\), the implementation first verifies the radical
grammar and computes its exact minimal polynomial. This must equal the monic
minimal polynomial of the target. Only then is
\code{Poly.same_root(e, target)} used to identify the embedding. The documented
operation compares two roots of a polynomial \cite{sympypolys}; its precondition
must not be replaced by numerical proximity to a root.

For example, \(\sqrt2+10^{-100}\) is extremely close to \(\sqrt2\), but is
not another representation of it. It is rejected before the root-separation
comparison. A wrong conjugate, such as \(-\sqrt2\), passes the minimal-
polynomial test but fails the selected-root test.

\paragraph{General towers.}
The in-memory certificate replays the output independently of the Galois
construction. It checks the root-of-unity register, every equation
\(C_j^{p_j}=a_j\), every principal-sector condition, and the final equality to
\(\alpha\) in the exact field. Conjugation, the cyclotomic register, and
the final target embedding are independently anchored by exact annihilation
followed by a known-root separation comparison; a field-membership conversion
is not trusted merely to have chosen some conjugate. Thus a bug in group selection or a bad
projector cannot be hidden by a merely plausible numerical answer.
The certificate reuses the number-field and root-isolation infrastructure; it
is an exact CAS certificate, not a Lean-checked proof.

The JSON certificate section is explicitly a \emph{summary}, not a standalone
serialized proof. It records the absolute field modulus, primitive expression,
prime indices, subgroup orders, and generator vectors for inspection. A fresh
process should either rerun the construction, verify the expanded radical
expression, or use the Wolfram exact check. No text in that summary is executed.

\section{Implementation architecture and data interchange}
\label{sec:implementation}
The Python code is divided along mathematical trust boundaries:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{.25\textwidth}p{.68\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Module & Responsibility \\\midrule
\code{program.py} & Radical grammar, straight-line programs, whitelisted JSON DAG, Wolfram rendering.\\
\code{groups.py} & Exact multiplication-table closure, commutators, derived series, prime-index series.\\
\code{field.py} & Embedded number-field arithmetic, automorphisms, coordinates, rational interval signs.\\
\code{solver.py} & Structural recognition, hint fields, negative tests, general tower construction, replay certificates.\\
\code{cli.py} & Strict request parsing, selected-root input, subprocess isolation and cancellation.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{A radical straight-line language}
A program consists of ordered assignments \(r_j:=E_j\), with each \(E_j\)
using only earlier registers, followed by an output expression. This preserves
sharing: a large radicand need not be copied at every use. The JSON transport
has only the operations \code{Q}, \code{I}, \code{Add}, \code{Mul}, and
\code{Pow}; rational numerators and denominators are decimal strings, and every
node reference points strictly backward. Rational-power denominators are
positive. Undefined and nonfinite values are rejected.

The Wolfram rendering uses nested \code{With}, not one simultaneous
\code{With} binding list whose right sides incorrectly assume sequential
scope. The JSON decoder builds arithmetic expressions directly. It never calls
\code{ToExpression} on backend output and never executes an arbitrary returned
source string. The human-readable \code{wolfram_program} is informational.

Expanding the DAG can still cause expression swell. The Python
\code{program.expression} size check is a convenience guard after
substitution, not a hard memory sandbox. The worker deadline is the reliable
cancellation boundary for expensive algebraic primitives; memory usage is not
hard-limited by this release.

\subsection{Root ordering across systems}
Python's direct API accepts an exact SymPy expression; the JSON API accepts a
polynomial with descending rational coefficients and a zero-based SymPy root
index. Wolfram uses its own root indexing, which must not be converted merely
by subtracting one for arbitrary complex inputs.

The Wolfram interface instead transmits a rationalized high-precision complex
approximation as a search hint. Python selects a likely conjugate, constructs
and certifies a result for that conjugate, and returns it. Wolfram then checks
\begin{lstlisting}
RootReduce[returnedExpression - originalRoot] === 0
\end{lstlisting}
with a separate verification limit. If this does not succeed, it increases the
hint precision and retries. An incorrect hint can therefore cause extra work
or an \code{Unknown} result, but not an accepted expression for the wrong root.
A negative Galois conclusion is independent of this conjugate choice because
the transmitted polynomial is the input's irreducible minimal polynomial.

\subsection{Dependency and operational limits}
The executed backend used Python and SymPy 1.14.0; the requirements pin that
SymPy version for reproducibility. It needs neither SageMath, GAP, Magma,
PARI/GP, nor an undocumented Wolfram function. SymPy supplies exact algebraic
primitives, while the arbitrary-degree embedded group construction is in this
package. Its optional tabulated-degree Galois precheck is not its general route.

The direct Python call has no implicit wall-clock deadline. The CLI starts a
worker subprocess and kills and reaps it on timeout, including on Windows.
The timeout covers the worker's algebraic work, not the parent interpreter's
initial startup. A field-degree check occurs \emph{after} a primitive-field
construction returns; an expensive field compositum may therefore consume the
time budget before its degree can be checked. These distinctions are visible
in the returned statuses.

\section{Using the package}
\label{sec:usage}
\subsection{Wolfram Language}
After extracting the archive, load the package by its path:
\begin{lstlisting}
Get["/path/to/RootRadicals/wolfram/RootRadicals.wl"];

a = Root[-1 - #^2 - #^3 + #^4 + #^6 &, 2];
e = RootToRadicals[a];
RootReduce[e - a] === 0
\end{lstlisting}
The two original examples have native structural paths. The general backend
requires Python with the accompanying dependency installed. A Windows
interpreter may be supplied explicitly; no shell quoting is required because
\code{RunProcess} receives an argument list.
\begin{lstlisting}
r = RadicalizeRoot[a,
  "PythonExecutable" -> "C:\\Python311\\python.exe",
  "UseNative" -> False,
  "TimeLimit" -> 180,
  "MaxFieldDegree" -> 64];

r["Status"]
r["Expression"]   (* only when Status is Success *)
\end{lstlisting}
Use \code{Method -> "Galois"} to force the explicit-field construction, or
\code{Method -> "Structural"} to stop after recognizers and hints. The strings
\code{"auto"}, \code{"galois"}, and \code{"structural"} are also accepted.
To remove the two backend policy limits:
\begin{lstlisting}
RadicalizeRoot[a, Method -> "Galois",
  "TimeLimit" -> Infinity, "MaxFieldDegree" -> Infinity]
\end{lstlisting}
Wolfram's separate native and verification limits remain in force unless
changed. The default verification limit is 60 seconds, and the default native
limit is 15 seconds. A returned \code{Success} always means the exact final
Wolfram equality check succeeded.

An extension hint is an expression, not a polynomial that defines an unknown
coefficient field:
\begin{lstlisting}
RadicalizeRoot[a, "ExtensionHints" -> {Sqrt[2], Sqrt[3]}]
\end{lstlisting}
Hints are optional accelerators and cannot make a nonsolvable number solvable.

\subsection{Python}
From the extracted project root:
\begin{lstlisting}
python -m pip install -e ./python
python -m unittest discover -s tests -v
\end{lstlisting}
Then:
\begin{lstlisting}
import sympy as s
from rootradicals import radicalize
x = s.Symbol("x")
a = s.CRootOf(x**6 + x**4 - x**3 - x**2 - 1, 1)
r = radicalize(a)
assert r.status == "success" and r.verify()
print(r.expression)
print(r.program.wolfram())
\end{lstlisting}
For the general route:
\begin{lstlisting}
r = radicalize(s.CRootOf(x**8 + 1, 7), method="galois")
assert r.verify()
print(r.details)                  # field degrees and prime indices
print(r.program.assignments)      # shared radical registers
print(r.program.output)
\end{lstlisting}
The CLI avoids arbitrary expression parsing. A minimal request is:
\begin{lstlisting}
{"coefficients":[["1","1"],["0","1"],["-2","1"]],
 "root_index":1,"method":"auto","time_limit":180,
 "max_field_degree":64}
\end{lstlisting}
Pass that JSON on standard input to \code{python python/driver.py}. Setting
\code{time_limit} and \code{max_field_degree} to JSON \code{null} removes the
corresponding guards. The direct Python API is for trusted SymPy expressions;
it is not an untrusted-text parser. Use the strict JSON protocol for external
inputs.

\section{Complexity and the next engineering steps}
\label{sec:complexity}
The bottleneck is the representation of the normal field, not the existence
of radicals. If \(n=\deg f\), then \(|G|\le n!\), and
\[
 D=[M:\Q]\le |G|\,\varphi(\rad |G|).
\]
This is an upper bound, not a forecast of typical field sizes. It nevertheless
explains why a root of modest degree can require an unwieldy absolute field.
The primitive element can have large coefficients even when \(D\) is small.
Factoring a degree-\(D\) polynomial over a degree-\(D\) field, constructing a
full multiplication table, and repeated rational linear systems magnify
this cost. The simple dense coordinate solver is also repeated rather than
incrementally maintained.

There are clear improvements that preserve the proof architecture. Field
bases could be reduced and coordinate factorizations cached. Several
projector values could be tried and scored for coefficient height instead of
choosing the first nonzero value. Small intermediate fixed fields could be
constructed using certified resolvents before forming a full absolute
splitting field. Repeated requests for different conjugates should share the
normal-field and tower construction, with only the final coordinate vector
changing. Native subfield and cyclotomic-conductor algorithms could replace
bounded pattern recognition for abelian examples.

For a production implementation aimed at difficult large-degree inputs,
Galois computation with local representations, invariant bounds, and exact
reconstruction is the more promising direction. Fieker and Sutherland's work
is a primary reference for that engineering path \cite{fiekersutherland}.
Magma's documented \code{SolveByRadicals} and \code{CyclicToRadical}
interfaces provide an existing implementation in that ecosystem
\cite{magma}. This package does not depend on Magma and does not claim to
match its performance.

Finally, minimizing radical depth or expression length requires a different
objective from merely constructing a radical tower. Different subgroup
chains, generator choices, and cyclotomic bases can produce very different
expressions. The supplied algorithm proves and computes existence, protects
the selected embedding, and exposes a straight-line representation suitable
for later denesting and simplification; it does not claim a canonical or
optimal normal form.

\section{General-route pseudocode}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
Radicalize(alpha):
    f := exact minimal polynomial of alpha over Q
    try structural candidates and certified hint-field reductions
    try exact inexpensive nonsolvability obstructions
    L := embedded splitting field of f
    m := product of distinct primes dividing [L:Q]
    M := L(zeta_m); B := Q(zeta_m)
    enumerate exact automorphisms of M fixing zeta_m
    H := their exact multiplication-table group
    if H is not solvable: return NotSolvable
    chain := H = H_0 > ... > H_s = 1, prime normal indices
    basis := [1, zeta_m, ..., zeta_m^(phi(m)-1)]
    registers := [r_0 = (-1)^(2/m)]
    for (H_j, N = H_(j+1)) in chain:
        p := |H_j| / |N|; choose sigma in H_j minus N
        for k = 0,...,[M:Q]-1:
            T := sum(tau(theta^k), tau in N)
            R := sum(zeta_p^(-i) * sigma^i(T), i=0,...,p-1)
            if R != 0: break
        a := reconstruct R^p in the current basis
        C := the unique R*zeta_p^(-i) in the principal sector
        emit r_(j+1) := a^(1/p), using earlier registers
        basis := [b*C^i for i=0,...,p-1 for b in basis]
    output := reconstruct alpha in the final basis
    replay radical equations, principal sectors, and target equality
    return Success(registers, output)
\end{lstlisting}
